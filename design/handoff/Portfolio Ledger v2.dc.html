Halving claim review time with a citation-first assist

Reviewers didn't need a model that decided. They needed one that could point at the paragraph.

shipped Meridian HealthSenior PMMar—Nov 20254 eng · 1 design · 2 SME

Claim reviewers at Meridian spent a median of eleven minutes per claim, and most of it was not judgement. It was reconciliation: holding a submitted invoice in one window and hunting the governing clause across a 40,000-page policy corpus in another.

The obvious pitch inside the company was auto-adjudication. Leadership had seen a vendor demo that decided low-value claims outright. I spent three weeks arguing against it, which is the part of this project I'd defend hardest.

What the evidence actually said

Fourteen shadowing sessions and a log study over 62,000 claims put the time cost in one place: reviewers re-read the same twelve clause families over and over, and they distrusted any summary they could not trace. In the survey (n=88), the top-ranked feature request was not speed. It was "show me where that came from."

Auto-adjudication also failed a constraint nobody had written down: our regulator requires a named human decision-maker per claim. A model that decides creates an audit hole. A model that retrieves does not.

What I built myself

I wrote the first working pipeline before engineering was staffed: clause-level chunking, hybrid retrieval, and an MCP server exposing the policy corpus and claims database as tools so the model could look things up instead of recalling them. Then the part that actually made the argument — a 240-case eval set graded by two SMEs, run on every prompt change.

That harness is what converted the auto-adjudication debate from opinion to numbers. Answer-level accuracy was fine at 0.94. Citation-span accuracy started at 0.71, and 0.71 is unshippable when the whole product promise is traceability. We spent six of the eight sprints on retrieval quality, not on the interface.

reviewer screen — citation span highlighted in policy pane

Where it landed

Median review time fell to five minutes twenty across 1,100 reviewers. The override rate — reviewers rejecting the assist's suggested clause — settled at 0.4%, and we kept publishing it internally as the honest quality metric rather than a satisfaction score.

problem{{ markProblem }}
11 min median per claim, spent reconciling invoices against a 40k-page policy corpus. Throughput capped hiring.
Reviewers handle 2.4M claims a year. At 11 minutes each, meeting the 2026 volume forecast meant hiring roughly 40 more reviewers — a request finance had already declined twice. The queue, not the decision quality, was the business problem.
evidence{{ markEvidence }}
14 shadowing sessions · log study, 62k claims · reviewer survey, n=88 · regulator constraint on named decision-maker.
Shadowing showed 62% of handling time in policy lookup, concentrated in twelve clause families. The log study confirmed it at scale: median 4.1 searches per claim, 71% of them repeats within the same shift. The survey ranked traceability above speed, 1st of 9 items. Compliance confirmed in writing that a model-issued decision would need a new control.
alternatives considered{{ markAlternatives }}
Clause macros · auto-adjudication · hiring 40 reviewers.
Clause macros. Cheapest option, and the SMEs wanted it. Rejected: plan-year drift broke 3 of 12 families annually, and someone has to own the rewrite.
Auto-adjudication. The exec favourite. Rejected: no named decision-maker, so every claim it touched became an audit exception.
Hire 40 reviewers. Fully costed at 3.1× the build over three years, and it scales the problem rather than removing it.
decision{{ markDecision }}
Retrieval assist with mandatory citation click-through. No model-made decisions, ever. Ship only above 0.95 span accuracy.
Signed off by the VP Claims and Compliance in the same room, which is why the guardrails held later. Three written commitments: the assist never writes a determination, the reviewer must open a citation before submitting, and any span-accuracy regression below 0.95 rolls the release back automatically.
outcome{{ markOutcome }}
Median 5m20 across 1,100 reviewers. 0.4% override rate. Passed the Q4 audit with no findings on the assist.
Rollout ran in four waves over ten weeks. Time savings held after the novelty period (week 12 median 5m34). What didn't move: appeal rates, unchanged at 2.8%, and reviewer satisfaction, up only 4 points — the job is still hard, it just has less clerical work in it.
lessons{{ markLessons }}
Citation UI mattered more than model choice. I under-scoped retrieval eval by about four weeks.
We swapped the underlying model twice with no measurable change in reviewer behaviour. Changing how spans were highlighted moved override rate by 1.9 points. Next time the eval harness gets built in week one, before the interface exists, and I'd write the rollback rule into the spec rather than negotiating it at launch.

I got into product through the back door.

I was a support engineer at a hotel software company in 2018, on the phone with night managers who had been handed a system nobody asked them about. I started writing up what they told me. Eventually someone noticed the write-ups were better than the roadmap and made me a PM, which I have never fully stopped feeling suspicious about.

Seven years later the shape of the job hasn't changed much: find the person doing the work, watch them do it, write down what's actually expensive. The difference is that I can now build the thing I'm describing. I taught myself Python badly in 2020 and less badly since, and in the last three years I've shipped retrieval pipelines, MCP servers and eval harnesses into production alongside engineers who were kind about my first pull requests.

That combination is mostly useful for one thing: arguing about AI features with evidence instead of vibes. When leadership wants the model to decide something, I can usually show them what happens when it does, in a week, on their real data.

I live in Lisbon with a very old greyhound. I run slowly, cook ambitiously, and keep a written log of every product decision I've regretted — the lab project on this site exists because that log was getting hard to search.

Things I'm bad at: hiring fast, sitting in silence when a meeting has clearly finished, and estimating retrieval work.
