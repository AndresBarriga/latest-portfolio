We had five products that all needed the same thing: who is staying, what they
booked, what packages they are entitled to. That data lives in the customer's
PMS, and every product was going to reach for it separately.

That framing matters, because the obvious reading is wrong. This was not a
performance problem. The API was fast enough and we were nowhere near rate
limits. It was a duplication problem. One integration per product per property
means the same onboarding work repeated five times, five caches with five
different refresh policies, and five separate places where the same failure can
appear.

So we built one service per PMS instance. It subscribes to Business Events from
the PMS and replicates reservations and profiles into our own infrastructure.
Any product that needs guest data reads from there. A customer running three of
our products integrates once.

## What we deliberately did not standardise

Worth being precise about who decided what. I owned the product boundary: which data the service replicates, what stays configurable per customer, and where the abstraction stops. Engineering owned how — the replication itself, the queue, the reconciliation, and everything about running it. The decisions that took the longest were the ones on the line between those, and they were made together.

Reference data is cached, and the refresh frequency is configurable by the
customer rather than fixed by us. Different properties tolerate stale data
differently, and it also depends on which product is consuming it. We set
sensible defaults for customers who do not want to think about it, but the
decision belongs to them. Choosing freshness on a customer's behalf is the kind
of default that looks tidy in design and generates support tickets for years.

The second thing that shaped the design is that a third-party system changes
underneath you without telling you. A property adds a payment method, renames a
package, reconfigures a rate. A product then sends a value the PMS rejects. The
naive behaviour is to return the error. Instead the service refreshes the
relevant dictionary, checks what the valid values actually are now, and either
retries with the correct value or falls back. Most of the time the failure
resolves itself rather than surfacing to a receptionist.

## Operating a replica, not just building one

Running a replica means owning its failure modes. Events arrive out of order, or
do not arrive. We built idempotent handling and retries, an event queue where
you can see what was processed, skipped or purged, scheduled reconciliation
against the source system, and a manual resync for reservations, profiles and
packages when someone needs to force the issue.

The observability mattered more than I expected. The difference between
replicating data and operating a replica is being able to answer why a booking
looks wrong without guessing.

What convinced me the abstraction was right is not the architecture. It is that
products got built on top of it that would have been hard to justify
individually: restaurant access control checking entitlements in real time,
pre-arrival communications driven by who is actually arriving tomorrow. The
integration stopped being a cost per product and became something products could
assume.
