I was the only product manager on this platform, owning the physician web
app with an internal team and the patient app with an external one. Two
numbers decided whether the service worked: how long clinicians spent on
each alert, and how many patients finished setting up. We measured both
before changing anything, and treated each step of the workflow as a
question: does this have to happen, and does a person have to do it?

## Clinician time

The service was only commercially viable if doctors could process alerts
quickly, so we measured average alert-handling time and agreed with
doctors what target would make it viable. That gave us a concrete number
to work backwards from, rather than a vague goal of "making the workflow
more efficient."

We then went through the workflow step by step to identify what actually
required clinical attention, what could be simplified and what could be
automated. We also ran workshops with nurses where we deliberately let
them use the product rather than explaining how we expected them to use
it. We observed where they hesitated and what they did differently from
our assumptions, used those observations to change the product, and
repeated the process.

The result: average alert-handling time down 40%.

## Alert triage: rules for what is dangerous, a model for what to read first

A separate design question was how much of alert triage should be decided
by rules and how much could be assisted by a model.

Letting a model decide what counts as dangerous was not defensible in a
regulated context: a missed threshold or a hallucinated reading is not an
acceptable failure mode. But rules alone left doctors reading raw data
with no prioritization, which was a large part of the alert-handling time.

We split it in two. A deterministic layer flagged dangerous readings with
fixed rules (a value rising by a set amount, or crossing a set
threshold): auditable and non-negotiable. We defined those rules together
with doctors, so the clinical judgment of what counts as dangerous stayed
with clinicians, not with product or engineering. On top of that, an LLM
layer ordered alerts by priority, highlighted which transmitted values
were potentially concerning, and produced a short summary of exactly what
to look at.

The model never decided what was dangerous. Its job was to reduce what a
doctor had to read before acting. Together with fewer screens and clicks
and all the necessary information in one place, that is where the
reduction came from.

## Patient onboarding

Onboarding had its own drop-off. Product analytics showed where patients
left, and we simplified the flow to fix those points.

We could not simply remove steps because they were inconvenient: some
existed because regulatory requirements demanded them. Instead, we looked
at where the burden could move from the patient to the product. If
something had to happen, the question became: does the patient actually
need to do it manually?

The result: onboarding completion time down 25% and completion rate up 18%.

## What it added up to

The product went from pre-launch to reimbursement-approved and live with
5 paying hospital customers, monitoring 500+ patients.
Regulation defined what had to happen; measuring a baseline told us where
the effort was being wasted. The habit that carried over: define the
constraint, measure the baseline, change the product, measure again.
