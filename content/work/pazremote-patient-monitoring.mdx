Building a remote patient monitoring product meant satisfying two very different definitions of "good."

The product had to meet regulatory requirements around how patients were onboarded, what was measured, how frequently measurements were taken and how accurate those measurements needed to be.

But meeting every requirement was not enough. Doctors also needed to process the resulting alerts within a reasonable amount of time. If managing one patient generated too much clinical work, the service would not be economically viable. We therefore measured average alert-handling time and discussed with doctors what target we needed to reach for the service to be profitable.

That gave us a concrete product constraint rather than a vague goal of "making the workflow more efficient."

We then went through the workflow step by step to identify what actually required clinical attention, what could be simplified and what could be automated. We also ran workshops with nurses where we deliberately let them use the product rather than explaining how we expected them to use it.

We observed where they hesitated, what they did differently from our assumptions and which parts of the workflow created unnecessary effort. We used those observations to change the product.

The same principle applied to patient workflows. We could not simply remove steps because they were inconvenient: some existed because regulatory requirements demanded them. Instead, we looked at where the burden could move from the user to the product. If something had to happen, the question became: does the patient or clinician actually need to do it manually?

This approach reduced average alert-handling time by 40%. More importantly, it changed how we thought about the product. Regulation was not a checklist sitting alongside the product — it defined constraints the product had to satisfy, while discovery with clinicians showed us how to satisfy those constraints without making the workflow unnecessarily heavy.

## Deterministic vs. AI-assisted triage

A separate design question was how much of alert triage should be decided by rules and how much could be assisted by a model.

Letting a model decide what counts as dangerous was not defensible in a regulated context: a missed threshold or a hallucinated reading is not an acceptable failure mode. But rules alone left doctors reading raw data with no prioritization, which was a large part of the alert-handling time.

We split it in two. A deterministic layer flagged dangerous readings with fixed rules (a value rising by a set amount, or crossing a set threshold): auditable and non-negotiable. We defined those rules together with doctors, so the clinical judgment of what counts as dangerous stayed with clinicians, not with product or engineering. On top of that, an LLM layer ordered alerts by priority, highlighted which transmitted values were potentially concerning, and produced a short summary of exactly what to look at.

The model never decided what was dangerous. Its job was to reduce what a doctor had to read before acting. Together with fewer screens and clicks and all the necessary information in one place, that is where the 40% came from.
