Two numbers decided whether this service worked: how long clinicians spent on
each alert, and how many patients finished setting up. We measured both before
changing anything, and treated each step of the workflow as a question: does
this have to happen, and does a person have to do it?

## The clinician-time target came from the economics

The service was only commercially viable if doctors could process alerts
quickly, so we measured average alert-handling time and agreed with doctors
what target would make it viable. That gave us a number to work backwards
from, rather than a vague goal of "making the workflow more efficient."

We went through the workflow step by step to find what actually required
clinical attention, what could be simplified and what could be automated.
**In workshops with nurses, we deliberately let them use the product instead of
explaining how we expected them to use it.** We watched where they hesitated
and what they did differently from our assumptions, changed the product, and
repeated the process.

The result: average alert-handling time down 40%.

## Alert triage: rules for what is dangerous, a model for what to read first

Letting a model decide what counts as dangerous was not defensible in a
regulated context: a missed threshold or a hallucinated reading is not an
acceptable failure mode. But rules alone left doctors reading raw data with no
prioritization, which was a large part of the alert-handling time.

So we split it in two. A deterministic layer flagged dangerous readings with
fixed rules (a value rising by a set amount, or crossing a set threshold):
auditable and non-negotiable. We defined those rules together with doctors, so
the clinical judgment of what counts as dangerous stayed with clinicians, not
with product or engineering. On top of that, an LLM layer ordered alerts by
priority, highlighted which transmitted values were potentially concerning,
and produced a short summary of exactly what to look at.

**The model never decided what was dangerous.** Its job was to reduce what a
doctor had to read before acting. Together with fewer screens and clicks and
all the necessary information in one place, that is where the reduction came
from.

## Regulation kept the steps; the product took over the work

Onboarding had its own drop-off. Product analytics showed where patients left,
and we simplified the flow at those points. We could not simply remove steps
because they were inconvenient: some existed because regulation demanded them.
So for each one, the question became: **does the patient actually need to do
it manually?**

The result: onboarding completion time down 25% and completion rate up 18%.

## Measure the baseline before you change anything

The product went from pre-launch to reimbursement-approved and live with 5
paying hospital customers, monitoring 500+ patients. **Regulation defined what
had to happen; measuring a baseline told us where the effort was being
wasted.**
