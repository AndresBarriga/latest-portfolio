GuestReg is a digital guest registration product. Instead of filling in a paper
registration card at the desk, the guest completes it themselves on a tablet or
their own phone, and the record syncs into the hotel's PMS as a guest profile.

Scanning identity documents had been one of the most persistent customer
requests across regions. The pull was operational rather than regulatory:
properties wanted faster check-in, fewer manual profile errors, and one vendor
covering registration and scanning instead of two. It had also stopped being a
nice-to-have. Several live opportunities were being evaluated against
competitors who already offered scanning, and for some prospects it had become a
requirement rather than an enhancement.

A technical spike compared four approaches and recommended buying. I agreed with
it at the time.

## Why building was ruled out first

The spike scored four options against speed to launch, cost, quality,
determinism, privacy and operational burden. Building from scratch came out best
on control, auditability and unit cost, and worst on everything to do with
delivery.

The objection was never writing an extraction engine. It was the tail. Identity
documents are a moving target: countries reissue formats, add security features,
change layouts. A vendor absorbs that continuously as part of the licence. Build
it yourself and you own a maintenance obligation that never ends and grows with
every market you sell into. For a team our size that is a permanent tax on the
roadmap, paid in engineering time forever, for a feature that was not the
product.

The AI options failed on a different axis. An external API is the fastest way to
a prototype, but identity documents leaving our environment is a hard
conversation with GDPR-sensitive customers, and a model that hallucinates a
passport number is worse than one that fails visibly. Self-hosting solves the
data question and replaces it with infrastructure and MLOps burden we had no
reason to take on. Both are non-deterministic where the entire value is
deterministic extraction of known fields.

So buy was the right recommendation on its own terms. Then I went and got the
pricing.

## The number that killed it

Our product is priced per room per month. Every vendor priced per scan.

I spoke to three vendors and modelled their plans against our licence. Using
occupancy data across the properties we had, a conservative estimate put scans
at eight per room per month. Even at that level, vendor rates consumed roughly
three quarters of total licence revenue for the room and exceeded the base
product licence on their own. Realistic volumes were considerably higher.

The ratio held across every property I modelled, because it depends on scans per
room rather than on the customer. This was not a negotiation problem. A
per-transaction cost sitting underneath a fixed recurring price is structurally
wrong, and no discount changes the shape of it.

So I went back and argued to reverse a decision that had already been approved.

## Asking the field before locking scope

Before committing to a scope I ran a discovery round with sales and service
delivery teams across two regions, asking what their properties actually needed
rather than what we assumed. Three things came back. Demand was operational, not
regulatory. Image retention requirements varied by country, so storage had to be
configurable rather than a fixed product decision. And in markets with heavy
domestic tourism, MRZ-only coverage would not be enough.

That last point became the trade-off I ended up accepting, knowing in advance
who it would disappoint.

## One standard instead of every format

Building full document coverage in-house was never realistic. Covering every
national ID format in the world is what the vendors exist for.

MRZ is the machine-readable strip on passports and on most European national ID
cards. It is a fixed international standard, it parses deterministically, and
there are mature open source libraries for it. It also covers a whole market
almost by construction: a European guest travels with an ID card carrying an MRZ
or with a passport, and a non-European guest always travels with a passport. So
European properties are covered, and so is any property with predominantly
international guests.

This is also what neutralised the maintenance objection from the spike. The tail
of country-specific format churn only exists if you try to read country-specific
formats. MRZ does not change.

What it does not cover is domestic travel in markets using non-MRZ national IDs.
The regional sales teams had flagged exactly this and were not happy with the
scope. They were right that the feature is much less useful to them. I still
think shipping a narrow version that worked was better than waiting for a broad
version we could not afford.

The privacy position fell out of the same choice. Extraction runs on the device
in about two seconds and we store the extracted fields, never the document
image. Some markets do require image retention, so storage will become a
per-property setting rather than a default.

## Phasing coverage instead of promising it

The scope was narrow, but the architecture was not. Extraction sits behind a
provider abstraction from day one, so MRZ, OCR and any future document
understanding are implementations behind a common interface rather than
competing rewrites. That was the condition for accepting a limited phase one:
narrow coverage, but no dead end.

Phase one shipped as MRZ plus manual confirmation. The guest opens GuestReg,
scans, the fields populate, and they review and correct before saving. Manual
entry remains available and is the fallback when a scan fails.
Registrations complete around 50% faster when the guest scans, and roughly half of eligible guests choose to scan rather than type. That adoption number is the one I watch: scanning is optional and manual entry is always available, so it measures whether guests actually prefer it rather than whether we shipped it.

Phase two is now underway. It adds OCR with per-country field rules for
documents without an MRZ, taught one country at a time rather than attempted
globally. What triggered it was not the roadmap: it was two specific customer
requests from Vietnam and Thailand, markets with heavy domestic travel, that
came in after those teams saw phase one working. The teams who had objected
loudest to the original scope are the ones now pulling the extension, which is
the outcome I wanted from shipping something narrow first.

Phase three, if the coverage keeps paying for itself, is document
classification: detect the document type automatically, extract to the same
structured object regardless of country, and map it into whichever PMS the
property runs.

What I got wrong is the sequence. Build versus buy was answered on technical
grounds and approved before anyone modelled what it would cost per room. The
commercial model should have come first.
