GuestReg is our digital guest registration product: the guest fills in the
registration card on a tablet or their own phone, and the record syncs into the
hotel's PMS. Scanning identity documents was one of the most persistent requests
across regions, and it was showing up in live deals as a reason to choose
competitors who already offered it.

## Buying was the right answer, until the pricing

A technical spike scored four options against speed to launch, cost, quality,
determinism, privacy and operational burden, and recommended buying a specialist
vendor. I agreed. Building in-house scored best on cost and control and worst on
delivery: identity documents are a moving target, and a vendor absorbs new
formats and security features as part of the licence. The AI options were faster
to prototype, but identity documents leaving our environment is a hard
conversation with compliance, and a model that hallucinates a passport number is
worse than one that fails visibly.

**Then I went and got the pricing.**

## Per-scan pricing broke the per-room licence

Our product is priced per room per month. Every vendor priced per scan. I
modelled three vendors' plans against our licence, using occupancy data across
our properties. Even at a conservative eight scans per room per month, vendor
rates consumed roughly 70% of the licence revenue for that room, more than the
base product licence on its own. Realistic volumes were higher.

The ratio held across every property, because it depends on scans per room, not
on the customer. **A per-transaction cost under a fixed recurring price is
structurally wrong, and no discount changes the shape of it.** So I argued to
reverse a decision that had already been approved.

## Discovery set the trade-off before I locked scope

I asked sales and service delivery teams in two regions what their properties
actually needed. Three things came back: demand was operational, not
regulatory; image retention rules varied by country, so storage had to be
configurable; and in markets with heavy domestic tourism, MRZ-only coverage
would not be enough. That last point became the trade-off I accepted, knowing in
advance who it would disappoint.

## One standard covers Europe, and the rest waits

MRZ is the machine-readable strip on passports and most European ID cards: a
fixed international standard that parses deterministically, with mature open
source libraries. In Europe a guest carries either an MRZ ID card or a passport,
so European properties are covered, and so is any property with mostly
international guests. Domestic travel in markets with non-MRZ national IDs is
not. The regional sales teams were right that this made the feature far less
useful to them. I still think a narrow version that worked beat waiting for a
broad one we could not afford.

## A narrow phase one, with no dead end

Extraction sits behind a provider abstraction from day one, so MRZ, OCR and any
future document understanding are implementations behind one interface rather
than competing rewrites. That was my condition for accepting narrow coverage.

Phase one shipped as MRZ plus manual confirmation: the guest scans, reviews the
fields and corrects them before saving, with manual entry as the fallback.
Registrations complete around 50% faster when guests scan, and roughly half of
eligible guests choose to scan. That adoption number is the one I watch:
scanning is optional, so it measures whether guests prefer it, not whether we
shipped it.

Phase two adds OCR with per-country field rules, one country at a time. It was
triggered by requests from Vietnam and Thailand after those teams saw phase one
working. **The teams who objected loudest to the original scope are the ones now
pulling the extension.**

## What I got wrong: the order

Build versus buy was answered on technical grounds and approved before anyone
modelled the cost per room. **The commercial model should have come first.**
