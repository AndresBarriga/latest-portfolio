We wanted guests to be able to say what they want. Book a spa treatment on the
17th. Bring champagne to the room. And we wanted staff to be able to ask about
their own operation across multiple products.

The information needed to answer any of that is spread across reservations, spa
availability, F&B and the documents each hotel uploads. But the interesting
requests are not questions. They are actions, and they land in a PMS and a POS
the customer owns and runs their business on.

## No central orchestrator: each product exposes what it already ships

The obvious design is one orchestrator that holds every integration and does the
writing. We did not build that.

Instead each product exposes its own MCP server, offering a narrow set of
operations it already implements: create a booking, place an order, move a
shift, reschedule a reservation. They are the operations the product already
ships and has tested heavily, exposed through a different door.

So a guest asking for champagne does not result in a write to the POS. It
results in an order in the ordering product, which then does what it already
does: the same path a tap in the interface would take. The product owns the
logic for getting that right, including everything it has learned about the
customer's configuration.

This is the part I would defend hardest. **The model chooses among operations;
it never composes one.** We have not seen hallucinated actions in production,
and the reason is structural rather than a matter of how well the prompt is
written.

## Most questions need documents, not operations

Not every question has an operation behind it. A guest asks what time the pool
closes, whether there is parking, how to get to the station. None of that lives
in a PMS.

So property documents are a source alongside the operational products. Hotel
admins upload their own PDFs and website content, and it becomes answerable in
the same conversation as a booking. Events showed why this matters: an
organiser uploads a schedule, and attendees can ask about their own sessions
without anyone building an event product.

This half costs almost nothing and covers most of the questions. The
operational half is harder and matters more, but **a system that can only act
and cannot answer is not something a guest would open twice.**

## A conversation is not consent: room charges need an OTP

Guests authenticate by QR from their reservation; staff log in. The two do not
see the same tools, because they are not talking to the same set of servers.

Payments were the case where identity by itself was not enough. A guest can pay
through Stripe or post a charge to the room, and posting to the room moves money
without a card present. That operation is exposed, but it always requires an OTP
to the guest's phone before it executes.

Everything else the assistant does is either reversible or low-consequence, and
the request itself is sufficient authorisation. A charge is neither, and **I did
not want the transcript to be the only evidence that a guest agreed to it.**

## No single service understands the whole system

That is the honest trade-off. Each product team owns its own exposed surface, so
adding a product means building its server rather than getting orchestration
for free. **That is a real maintenance cost and I do not want to claim
otherwise.**

What we get for it is that capability grows with the portfolio. Each product
added extends what can be asked and done, and the write path for every new
capability is one a product team already owns and tests, rather than one that
has to be re-earned in a central place.
