We wanted guests to be able to say what they want. Book a spa treatment on the
17th. Bring champagne to the room. And we wanted staff to be able to ask about
their own operation rather than click through four products to assemble an
answer.

The information needed to answer any of that is spread across reservations, spa,
F&B, and whatever the property has uploaded — their own PDFs and website content
that hotel admins add. But the interesting requests are not questions. They are
actions, and they land in a PMS and a POS the customer owns and runs their
business on.

## Where the boundary went

The obvious design is one orchestrator that holds every integration and does the
writing. We did not build that.

Instead each product exposes its own MCP server, offering a narrow set of
operations it already implements: create a booking, place an order, move a shift,
reschedule a reservation. These are not new capabilities written for the
assistant. They are the operations the product already ships and has been tested
on heavily, exposed through a different door.

So a guest asking for champagne does not result in a write to the POS. It results
in an order in the ordering product, which then does what it already does — the
same path a tap in the interface would take. The product owns the logic for
getting that right, including everything it has learned about the customer's
configuration.

This is the part I would defend hardest. The model chooses among operations; it
never composes one. There is no path where it constructs a novel write against a
customer's system, because the only things it can invoke are things that already
existed and were tested. We have not seen hallucinated actions in production, and
the reason is structural rather than a matter of how well the prompt is written.
The other half is not operational at all

Not every question has an operation behind it. A guest asks what time the pool closes, whether there is parking, how to get to the station. A conference attendee asks where the mentorship talks are and when they start. None of that lives in a PMS.

So property documents are a source alongside the operational products. Hotel admins upload their own PDFs and website content, and it becomes answerable in the same conversation as a booking. Events turned out to be the case that showed why this matters: an organiser uploads a schedule, and attendees can ask about their own sessions without anyone building an event product. The property added the content; we added nothing.

This is the half that costs almost nothing and covers most of the questions. The operational half is harder and matters more, but a system that can only act and cannot answer is not something a guest would open twice.

## Identity, and the one case that needed more

Guests authenticate by QR from their reservation; staff log in. The two do not
see the same tools, because they are not talking to the same set of servers.

Payments were the case where identity by itself was not enough. A guest can pay
through Stripe or post a charge to the room, and posting to the room moves money
without a card present. That operation is exposed, but it always requires an OTP
to the guest's phone before it executes.

The reasoning is that a conversation is not consent. Everything else the
assistant does is either reversible or low-consequence, and the request itself is
sufficient authorisation. A charge is neither, and I did not want the transcript
to be the only evidence that a guest agreed to it.

## What it costs

The honest trade-off is that no single service understands the whole system. Each
product team owns its own exposed surface, which means adding a product means
building its server rather than getting orchestration for free. That is a real
maintenance cost and I do not want to claim otherwise.

What we get for it is that capability grows with the portfolio. Each product
added extends what can be asked and done, and the write path for every new
capability is one a product team already owns and tests, rather than one that has
to be re-earned in a central place.
