---
title: 'Internal Product Documentation Chatbot'
slug: 'ask-ai-productinfo'
problem: >
  Product knowledge (installation, configuration, troubleshooting,
  roadmap) lives scattered across Confluence, exported PDFs, and the
  heads of a few people. Support and CS — the people who need it
  most — don't have access to those tools or a fast way to find
  answers. The same questions get answered by hand, repeatedly, over
  Slack and email.
decision: >
  Strict per-product isolation instead of global search: each product
  is its own compartment with its own documents and conversation
  history, and the filter is applied before ranking, not after. This
  is the opposite of what mature systems in this category do, which
  search across the whole company corpus.
  I deliberately rejected the company's existing product stack
  (heavier, built for a different scale) in favor of Python +
  FastAPI + SQLite at prototype stage — infrastructure that added
  nothing at the real scale (857 chunks) and would have blocked
  getting started.
  I switched from a larger model to a smaller one after measuring the
  trade-off: 3x cheaper, median latency down from 5.60s to 3.24s,
  groundedness intact, one point of completeness lost. I rejected
  routing between models based on question complexity — the
  heuristics didn't hold up, and the real failure was in the prompt,
  not the model's capability.
rigor: >
  Five concrete findings broke my initial assumptions:

  (1) Missing heading path. Problem: the highest-impact fix wasn't visible in any test. Measurement: "install" showed up in 4 of 42 chunks when searching embedded text, but 28 of 42 once the heading path was included. Change: embedding fragment text alone had silently dropped the page title; adding the heading path took retrieval of the installation guide from 1/10 to 9/10, and an early 23-question eval from 22/23 to 23/23.

  (2) Wrong problem, wrong fix. Problem: 3 facts kept getting missed. Measurement: two complete mechanisms, built to fix what looked like a missing-information problem, together moved only 1 of the 3. Change: widening the retrieval window to 50 for diagnosis showed the missing facts sitting at ranks 11, 12, and 12 — the real bottleneck was K=10, never questioned before building on top of it.

  (3) Sync bug invisible from the UI. Problem: updating one documentation branch silently deleted the previous branches for the same product. Measurement: the UI kept showing all branches as correct while only the most recently synced documents actually survived. Change: replace scope moved from product-level to branch-level.

  (4) Corrupted, not unparseable. Problem: an exported PDF arrived with zero extractable characters. Measurement: 62% of the file was Unicode replacement-character sequences — a lossy conversion in transport, not a parsing-library failure. Change: ingesting directly from the source API moved from a "maybe later" idea to the primary path. (Mechanical text-matching checks had the same failure mode elsewhere — false positives and negatives, including one Unicode-escape test that passed green while testing nothing real.)

  (5) A harness that measures something real. Problem: relying on gut feel about whether a change helped. Measurement: a separate, later 21-case harness (graded by a judge model different from the one answering) produced a non-monotonic history — the sign it's measuring something real, not noise: baseline 85.7% correct; K 6→10 brought it to 100%; switching to a cheaper generation model made it drop to 95.2% — a real, measured regression; completeness rules in the prompt recovered it to 100%. Change: the final 3-repetition confirmation run stopped at 7 of 21 cases when the API account ran out of credit — logged as incomplete, not reported as 100%.
traction: >
  Shown to several people in Support, Product and CS internally; positive
  reaction, and they're evaluating it. Approved by Product Director to keep going. Now deployed to stage environment and people actively using it.
nextVersion: >
  Diagnose before building: two complete mechanisms were measured and
  discarded, because nobody first checked where the missing facts
  actually ranked. The rule I take from this: when a retrieval case
  fails, the first question is "where is the missing information
  ranked?" — not "what mechanism should we build?"

  The evaluation set was also initially built around a single
  document and saturated at 100% — it stopped distinguishing a good
  configuration from a better one. Expanding it with varied content
  should have come before several of the optimization experiments.

  Immediate, concrete next step: finish the 3-repetition confirmation
  across all 21 cases — it stopped at 7 for lack of API credit, not
  by choice.
---

This started inside a hospitality SaaS platform, where product
knowledge — installation, configuration, troubleshooting, the
roadmap — was scattered across Confluence, exported PDFs, and the
heads of a handful of people. Support and CS, who needed it most,
didn't have access to those tools or a fast way to search them. The
same questions kept getting answered by hand, over Slack and email,
one ticket at a time.

Company and product names are omitted throughout — the underlying
documentation spans several internal products, and nothing below
depends on which ones.
