Writing a user story or a PRD used to mean describing a product I could only
imagine. I wrote what I thought we wanted, handed it over, and found out weeks
later whether the thing in my head and the thing in everyone else's were the same
product. Usually they were close. Close is expensive.

Now the first artefact is a working prototype I build myself. Not a mockup,
something you can click through.

## The two things that actually changed

Everything else here follows from these.

**Validation moved before the build, not after it.** We put something usable in
front of customers while changing it still costs an afternoon. People are polite
about descriptions and specific about things they can hold, so the feedback is
different in kind, not only earlier. Features die at this point, which is the
whole idea. One that dies in a demo costs a prototype, one that dies in
production costs a quarter.

**Handover became showing instead of explaining.** Developers receive the
prototype alongside the PRD, so they read a document about something they have
already used. That gap between what I wrote and what someone else pictured is
where most of the waste used to live.

<Callout>
**Build to learn, not to earn.**

The prototype exists to answer questions: is this valuable, will people use
it this way, does it work as a business. Engineers build to earn, and they
are better at it than they have ever been. My job is to make sure that what
they build is worth building.
</Callout>

## Who receives what

**Me.** I build it, look at it, and often decide it is wrong before anyone else
has spent an hour on it. That filter did not exist before. I could not properly
evaluate my own idea until it was built, which meant I evaluated it once it was
already expensive.

**Design.** UI/UX used to be a dependency at the start of the work. Now there is
something concrete to react to, so the review happens against a real artefact
rather than a description. The role moved from producing the starting point to
improving one.

**Engineering.** We walk through the prototype together on a call, and they write
the TRD and implementation plan from a shared reference rather than from my
prose. The questions that come back are about edge cases rather than about what I
meant.

**Customers.** This is the one that changed most. Being able to show a hotel
manager a working flow gets a different answer than describing one. And we only
build once we are reasonably sure it will deliver real value.

## The process

An idea becomes a clickable prototype in low-code, which goes through internal
review and then in front of customers. That loop runs as many times as it needs
to. Only once it holds up do I write the PRD, which means the PRD describes
something that has already survived contact with the people who will use it.

The handover call is the point engineering commits. Developers write the TRD and
implementation plan, with an agent checking the TRD against the PRD and flagging
what is unclear before anyone builds on top of an ambiguity. Work breaks down
into Jira stories, agents implement and report back on the ticket, and a second
agent reviews the diff independently before a developer sees it.

Then it goes back in front of customers, before pilot and before production.
Validation is not a phase that closes.

## It scales down

The full loop is what a new product gets. Most work is not a new product.

For a single feature or a change to an existing screen, the same thing happens in
compressed form: prototype the change, show it to whoever asked for it, adjust,
then hand it over. It might be an afternoon rather than several weeks. What stays
constant is the order — something clickable exists before anything is specified,
and someone outside the team has reacted to it before engineering commits.

This is now how the portfolio works rather than an experiment on one product. The
scale changes; the sequence does not.

## One example: TYNGO Spa

_One product, not a pattern I can generalise from. It is the clearest case I
have._

We built digital check-in, advanced analytics, a customisable booking flow and an
automated waitlist. Almost none of it survived contact with the people who would
use it.

The configurable booking flow looked like a selling point and came back as work:
twenty options to set up is not flexibility, it is homework. They wanted good
defaults. Automated upselling during booking failed differently — pushing extras
made a premium reservation feel like an online store. Managers did not open most
of the analytics. Reception did not want digital check-in taking away the human
moment. VIP guests turned out to need manual handling by design rather than by
omission.

What we built instead came from watching what reception actually does all day: an
operational day board that runs the whole shift from one screen, scheduling that
understands therapist and room and equipment rather than just availability,
treatment packages, and cancellation recovery. One-click rebooking of the same
treatment with the same therapist in four weeks is not an exciting feature.
Reception uses it constantly.

## What came out of it

**Fewer features built that nobody wanted.** Four rejected directions on one
product, all caught before engineering committed. That is the outcome I care most
about, and it is invisible in any roadmap because it shows up as work that never
happened.

**Better acceptance of what did ship.** The replacement features came out of
watching the work rather than guessing at it, and reception uses them daily
rather than being trained into them.

**Faster delivery, for a specific reason.** Duty Roster went from nothing to
production in under two months. Not because code got written faster, but because
we were not building the wrong version of it and nobody had to rework a
misunderstanding.

**Less translation loss.** Design reviews a real artefact. Developers ask about
edge cases instead of intent. I catch my own bad ideas early.

## What did not change

The prototype is not the architecture. It is a communication artefact, and
treating it as a foundation would be a mistake. Engineering rebuilds properly
from the PRD and TRD.

And the review question is open. The developer reads a summary rather than
several hundred lines. That is faster, and there is still a human in the loop,
but I am not going to pretend it is free. Reviewing a summary changes what you
are looking at, and I do not think anyone knows yet what that costs. It is the
question I would want answered before leaning on this any harder.
